# run-610: Multi-Projection Synchronization, Effort & Single-Lane Timeview

**Kind:** interaction-lab  
**Port:** 3610  
**DB:** none  
**Question:** How does client-side reactivity maintain 60 FPS synchronization across three simultaneous projections (Kanban board, flat list, and compact single-lane Timeview) when items are dragged or mutated, and does a concrete time slot allocation ("to-do-when") alongside effort and capacity indicators render abstract priority numbers and separate due dates redundant?

## Scenario

A developer with a systems and C/C++ background is investigating how to represent work across time, status, and tabular projections without UI jank, cascading re-renders, or cognitive overload. In desktop and tablet interfaces, users frequently need to observe the same collection of items through different conceptual lenses: a status-oriented 3-column **Kanban board** (`backlog`, `in progress`, `done`), a dense sortable **Flat List**, and Lerch's compact horizontal **Timeview** lane layout.

The developer launches the interactive lab at `http://localhost:3610` (`DB=none`), initialized with a realistic seeded dataset of ~30–50 engineering tasks carrying `title`, `effort` (estimated hours), `scheduled_time` (`start` and `end` timestamps), `state` (`unstarted`, `started`, `done`, `discarded`), and `kanban` column placement.

Through the diagnostic workbench, the developer evaluates four interconnected interaction mechanics:

1. **Simultaneous Multi-Projection Synchronization & Reactivity Profiling (Original `run-602`):**
   The developer inspects items rendered simultaneously across three live panels:
   - *Kanban Board:* Standard cards partitioned across `backlog`, `in progress`, and `done` columns with drag-and-drop movement.
   - *Flat List:* A dense tabular view showing item titles, state indicators, column placement, effort values, and scheduled timestamps.
   - *Single-Lane Timeview:* A horizontal timeline where items are positioned along the time axis according to their `scheduled_time`.
   When an item is dragged to a new column on the Kanban board, its state updates immediately in the Flat List and its card re-renders on the Timeview. Similarly, dragging a time block along the Timeview axis or resizing its duration instantaneously updates the item's schedule and effort in the List and Kanban views. A real-time Telemetry HUD tracks frame render durations (`requestAnimationFrame`), Vue reactive dependency trigger counts, and paint latencies to detect whether updates cause wasteful virtual DOM recalculations or frame drops.

2. **The Single-Lane Horizontal Timeview & Dynamic Stacking (`§2.6 Q2`):**
   The developer examines Lerch's compact Timeview layout algorithm:
   - *Single-Lane Compression:* As long as scheduled items do not overlap in time, they compress into a single horizontal timeline lane, preserving vertical screen space for other workspace tools.
   - *Dynamic Conflict Stacking:* When two or more items have conflicting `scheduled_time` windows, the lane dynamically branches and stacks vertically into sub-lanes only for the duration of the overlap, visually exposing calendar clashes.
   - *Direct Manipulation:* Dragging a block along the time axis shifts its start and end times; dragging an edge resize handle adjusts its estimated duration (`effort`).
   The developer compares this compact single-lane layout against traditional multi-row calendar grids and heavy Gantt charts to judge spatial economy and daily planning clarity.

3. **"To-Do-When" vs. Abstract Due Dates & Priorities (`§2.4 Q1`, `§2.4 Q2`):**
   The developer tests the core Lerch scheduling hypothesis: *Does allocating an item directly onto a concrete time slot ("to-do-when") naturally replace disconnected due dates and abstract priority numbers (`P0`–`P4`, `High`/`Low`)?*
   An interactive comparison toggle switches between two scheduling models:
   - *Mode A (Legacy Explicit Attributes):* Items carry independent `priority` tags and separate `due_date` deadlines. The developer experiences common planning pitfalls: high-priority items lingering unscheduled, tasks slipping past due dates without clear schedule adjustments, and visual clutter from competing badge indicators.
   - *Mode B (First-Class "To-Do-When" Allocation):* Items carry only `effort` and a `scheduled_time` slot. Urgency is communicated intuitively by placement on the time axis. When too much work is scheduled for a given day, the interface prompts rescheduling rather than artificially labeling multiple items as "urgent".
   The developer evaluates whether Mode B produces clearer planning decisions with fewer contradictory states.

4. **Workload Capacity & Personal Availability Windows (`§2.4`):**
   The developer overlays personal availability windows (e.g., standard 8-hour working hours, lunch pauses, off-hours) on the Timeview timeline. Above each day column, a **Workload Capacity Bar** aggregates total scheduled `effort` against available capacity:
   - When scheduled work fits within daily availability (e.g. $\le 8\text{ h}$), the capacity gauge remains green/neutral.
   - When scheduled items exceed availability (>100%) or generate overlapping clashes, the capacity gauge turns amber/red, and conflict indicators prompt the user to re-balance or push back items.

Administrative controls, database persistence (PostgreSQL/MongoDB), WebSocket synchronization (handled in `run-603`), user authentication, and third-party calendar integrations (Google Calendar / CalDAV) are intentionally omitted to keep this prototype strictly focused on client reactivity performance, single-lane packing mechanics, and scheduling interaction ergonomics.

## What to Evaluate

### 1. Visual / Presentation Aspects
- **Single-Lane Packing Economy:** How effectively does the compact Timeview preserve vertical screen real estate compared to standard multi-lane Gantt or calendar views, and does dynamic stacking on temporal overlap feel visually clear or chaotic?
- **Clash & Overload Salience:** Visual distinctiveness of calendar conflicts (e.g. stacked blocks with warning accents) and capacity overruns on the daily workload capacity bar (color transitions from green to amber/red).
- **Cross-Projection Visual Tethering:** Visual feedback (such as synchronized hover highlights and selection outlines) that connects the same item across the Kanban board, Flat List, and Timeview without causing visual noise.
- **Diagnostic Telemetry HUD:** Real-time presentation of FPS, frame paint latency (ms), and reactive update counts without interfering with primary workspace layout.

### 2. Procedural / Workflow Aspects
- **"To-Do-When" Keystroke & Interaction Efficiency:** Comparing the operational steps of dragging an unscheduled backlog item onto the Timeview timeline versus opening modal forms to input abstract priority levels and separate due dates.
- **Clash Resolution Friction:** Experiencing how intuitively dragging overlapping blocks apart resolves calendar conflicts and restores the single-lane compression.
- **Feasibility Feedback Loop:** Evaluating whether capacity bars and availability boundaries provide immediate psychological feedback on planning feasibility, discouraging over-commitment compared to static due dates.
- **Multi-Projection Layout Modes:** Evaluating whether developers prefer working in a 3-pane synchronized dashboard or quick keyboard-switched views (e.g. `1` for Kanban, `2` for List, `3` for Timeview).

### 3. Technology / Stack Choices (Libraries, DX & Ergonomics)
- **Vue 3 / Nuxt 4 Reactivity Architecture:** Evaluating how fine-grained reactivity (`ref`, `computed`, `shallowRef`) handles concurrent updates across three complex views. Does updating an item property in one projection trigger excessive virtual DOM re-renders in sibling components?
- **Single-Lane Interval Packing Algorithm:** Assessing the runtime performance and implementation complexity of interval-scheduling / interval-graph coloring algorithms implemented in TypeScript running synchronously in the browser UI thread.
- **Drag-and-Drop Implementation Ergonomics:** Comparing drag-and-drop mechanics (e.g. HTML5 Drag and Drop API, Pointer Events, or lightweight Vue drag libraries like `@formkit/drag-and-drop` / `vue-draggable-plus`) for combined discrete column dropping (Kanban) and continuous timeline positioning (Timeview).
- **Frame Budget & Scale Stress Testing:** Measuring frame stability against the 16.6ms (60 FPS) budget during active drag operations at 30–50 items, as well as under a toggleable 250-item synthetic stress test.

## Done When

- [ ] Application starts cleanly at `http://localhost:3610` (`DB=none`) with pre-seeded datasets of ~30–50 items populated with `effort`, `scheduled_time`, `state`, and `kanban` columns.
- [ ] Three distinct projections render the same item collection simultaneously: a 3-column Kanban board (`backlog`, `in progress`, `done`), a sortable tabular Flat List, and the compact horizontal Timeview.
- [ ] The Timeview layout engine successfully packs non-overlapping items into a single horizontal lane, dynamically stacking items into sub-lanes only where temporal overlaps occur to expose calendar clashes.
- [ ] Mutating or dragging an item in any projection (e.g., column move in Kanban, effort edit in List, or time shift in Timeview) updates all other projections instantaneously without jank or full re-renders.
- [ ] An interactive comparison toggle switches between **Mode A** (legacy explicit `priority` + detached `due_date`) and **Mode B** (first-class `scheduled_time` + `effort`), demonstrating workflow differences and visual clarity.
- [ ] Personal availability windows and daily workload capacity bars visually display total scheduled `effort` against available capacity, triggering visual warnings when days exceed 100% capacity.
- [ ] A built-in Telemetry HUD displays real-time frame rates (FPS), frame render latency (ms), and reactive update counts, with a toggleable 250-item stress test verifying 60 FPS interaction stability.
- [ ] Scoped documentation (`docs/run-610/design.md`, `frontend.md`, `backend.md`, `NOTES.md`) is maintained.
